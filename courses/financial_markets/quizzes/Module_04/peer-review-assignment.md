# Housing: A Human Right Under Threat

The United Nations Human Rights Council's 2017 report, presented by the Special Rapporteur on adequate housing, powerfully argues that the financialization of housing represents a profound threat to human rights. I agree with the report's central premise that the housing sector has been fundamentally transformed by global financial actors, shifting its value from a human dwelling to a means of wealth accumulation, thereby perversely worsening fundamental economic and social problems.

The report outlines how housing has become "financialized", valued as a commodity and security for financial instruments traded globally, rather than a place for living. The scale of this transformation is staggering, with residential real estate comprising $163 trillion — 75 percent of all global real estate assets, and more than twice the world’s total GDP. Banks, pension and hedge funds, and private equity firms increasingly view housing in "hedge cities" as a safe haven for excess capital, often benefiting from tax shelters and a lack of transparency.

From the perspective of financial markets, the theoretical purpose is to efficiently allocate capital, facilitate investment, and promote economic growth. However, the report demonstrates how the financialization of housing perverts these functions, leading to significant societal downside:

Firstly, there's a distorted resource allocation and unaffordability. Instead of directing capital towards meeting the fundamental need for affordable housing, financial markets channel vast amounts of capital into speculative investments. This influx causes housing prices to skyrocket, often by over 50% in five years in some cities, making them unaffordable for most residents, including middle-income households. This is a severe misallocation of resources, prioritizing financial returns over the basic human right to shelter.

Secondly, there's market instability and social catastrophe. While financial markets are meant to manage risk, the financialization of housing has created extreme instability. When housing prices plummet, residents face mortgage foreclosure and homelessness on a massive scale, as seen in the U.S. (over 13 million foreclosures leading to more than 9 million evictions) and Spain (over half a million foreclosures resulting in over 300,000 evictions) following the financial crisis. This illustrates how financialization, left unregulated, can lead to widespread social catastrophe, with states often favoring financial institutions over their citizens' human rights.

Thirdly, there's exacerbated inequality and erosion of public good. The report identifies a "threefold assault on human rights" caused by financialization. Firstly, it undermines democratic governance and community accountability, with governments often responding to demands from credit agencies rather than human rights, and decisions made by remote financial entities. Secondly, it exacerbates inequality and social exclusion, creating more wealth for the wealthy while depriving the poor of housing and communities, promoting gentrification, and displacing marginalized groups like indigenous peoples, people with disabilities, women, and migrants. Thirdly, it detaches housing from its connection to communities and human dignity, as it becomes a dehumanized speculative commodity, making accountability for human rights violations difficult.

The report rightly contends that a "fundamental shift" is required for States to uphold their international human rights obligations, urging them to insist that markets serve the social function of housing rather than merely allowing markets to operate under their own rules.States are obligated to regulate and direct private market and financial actors to ensure that their actions align with the right to adequate housing for all sectors of society, not just the wealthy. Additionally, courts, tribunals, and national human rights institutions must finance laws.

In essence, the report compellingly argues that when housing is primarily treated as a financial asset rather than a human right, the very mechanisms intended to foster economic well-being can instead perversely deepen inequality, create instability, and undermine democratic accountability, making housing inaccessible and insecure for millions globally. The call for a paradigm shift is crucial to reorient financial markets to serve the fundamental human right to adequate housing.
